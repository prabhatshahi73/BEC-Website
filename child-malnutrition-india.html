India's Nutrition Reckoning: What the Data Actually Says About Feeding and Saving a Billion Children

Fewer Indian children are dying before their fifth birthday than at any point in the country's history. Yet nearly one in three is still stunted, and the country ranks among the hungriest in the world. A hard look at the numbers behind India's most consequential public health story — and how far it still has to travel.

By Prabhat Kumar Shahi Environmental Consultant & Founder, Bharat Environment Consultants 12 min read

There are two Indias visible in the country's child health data right now, and both are true at the same time. One is a genuine, hard-won success story: an infant mortality rate that has been cut nearly in half in a decade, a sex ratio at birth climbing steadily toward parity, and a maternal mortality ratio that has fallen by almost a third. The other is a stubborn, entrenched crisis: a nutrition burden so large that India still carries the world's highest concentration of wasted and stunted children, and a Global Hunger Index score that places it in the "serious" category alongside some of the world's poorest nations.

Both pictures are accurate. Neither cancels the other out. Understanding modern India's public health trajectory means holding these two facts together — real, measurable progress on child survival, running in parallel with a nutrition crisis that survival gains alone have not solved. This is not a story that fits neatly into either triumphalism or despair. It fits into the data, which is where this account begins.

The Survival Story: A Genuine, Measurable Success

Start with what has gone right, because it is substantial and it is real. According to the Sample Registration System (SRS) Statistical Report, India's Infant Mortality Rate — the probability that a child born in a given year dies before turning one — has fallen from 39 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2014 to 24 in the most recent reporting period. The Under-Five Mortality Rate, a broader measure of child survival, has dropped over the same period from 45 to 28 per 1,000 live births. The Neonatal Mortality Rate, capturing deaths in a baby's first 28 days, fell from 26 to 19 per 1,000 live births as of the 2021 SRS report, with further gains recorded since.

Figure 1. India's Infant Mortality Rate and Under-Five Mortality Rate have both declined by more than a third over the past decade. Data: Sample Registration System Reports 2014, 2021, 2024, Office of the Registrar General of India.

Put in global context, these numbers represent one of the more significant public health achievements of the past generation anywhere in the world. The United Nations Inter-agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation's 2024 report found that India achieved a 78% decline in its Under-Five Mortality Rate between 1990 and 2023, comfortably outpacing the global average decline of 61% over the same period. Neonatal mortality fell 70% against a global average of 54%, and infant mortality fell 71% against a global 58%. This is not marginal progress — it reflects the cumulative effect of expanded institutional delivery, immunisation coverage under Mission Indradhanush, and a maternal and child health infrastructure built over three decades.

The Maternal Mortality Ratio tells a similarly encouraging story: it declined from 130 deaths per 100,000 live births in 2014–16 to 93 in 2019–21, according to the Special Bulletin on Maternal Mortality based on SRS data — a reduction of nearly 30% in five years. And in a statistic with deep social significance, the Sex Ratio at Birth — long a marker of entrenched gender bias in parts of the country — has improved from 899 females per 1,000 males in 2014 to 918 in the most recent SRS reporting, continuing a gradual but consistent upward trend.

Why Survival Gains Happened

These improvements did not happen by accident. Institutional delivery rates — the share of births taking place in a hospital or health facility rather than at home — have risen sharply under schemes like Janani Suraksha Yojana, which provides cash incentives for institutional births, and Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram, which removes out-of-pocket costs for delivery care. Immunisation coverage under Mission Indradhanush has pushed full immunisation rates for children well above historical levels. Expanded access to skilled birth attendants and postnatal care has directly reduced the neonatal deaths that once accounted for the majority of infant mortality in India.

The Nutrition Story: Where Progress Has Been Real But Insufficient

Set against this backdrop of survival gains, India's nutrition indicators present a more complicated picture — genuine improvement, but from a starting point so severe that the country remains an outlier even among nations with comparable income levels. NFHS-6, covering 2023–24 and released by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare in May 2026, found that child stunting — low height for age, a marker of chronic undernutrition — declined to 29.3% from 35.5% in NFHS-5. That means just under one in three Indian children under five remains stunted, down from just over one in three previously.

Figure 2. Stunting has fallen meaningfully across three rounds of the National Family Health Survey. Wasting and underweight prevalence have moved far more slowly. Data: NFHS-4 (2015–16), NFHS-5 (2019–21), NFHS-6 (2023–24), Ministry of Health & Family Welfare.

Wasting — low weight for height, a marker of acute, short-term undernutrition often triggered by illness or sudden food insecurity — has barely moved. It stood at 21.0% in NFHS-4, 19.3% in NFHS-5, and roughly 19% in NFHS-6, with severe wasting dropping more meaningfully from 7.7% to 5.2%. Underweight prevalence among children under five has similarly stagnated, declining only marginally from 32.1% to 31.8% across the two most recent survey rounds. These indicators — unlike stunting, which reflects cumulative, longer-term nutritional history — respond quickly to shocks: a poor harvest, a bout of diarrhoeal illness, a household income disruption. Their persistence suggests that the underlying vulnerability to acute malnutrition has not meaningfully declined, even as chronic stunting has.

The First 1,000 Days — And Where They Go Wrong

Public health researchers increasingly frame the period from conception to a child's second birthday — roughly 1,000 days — as the decisive window in which nutritional deficits become largely irreversible. NFHS-6's own infant and young child feeding data show why this window remains India's weakest link: only about half of newborns are breastfed within the first hour of birth, despite overwhelming evidence that early breastfeeding initiation reduces neonatal infection and mortality risk. Only around 60% of children aged 6–8 months receive solid or semi-solid complementary foods on time. And most strikingly, just 15% of children aged 6–23 months receive what NFHS-6 defines as an adequate diet — meaning the overwhelming majority of India's youngest children are not eating what their own government's health survey considers minimally sufficient for healthy development.

This data point deserves to sit at the centre of any serious conversation about Indian child health, because it reframes the problem. India's nutrition crisis is not primarily a story about food scarcity at the national level — the country is a net food exporter and has not experienced famine-level shortages in decades. It is a story about dietary quality, feeding practices, and the distribution of adequate nutrition within households and across the critical early years, compounded by low consumption of pulses, fruits, vegetables, nuts, and animal-source proteins that NFHS-6 documents across the population.

Where the Numbers Diverge: Regional and Rural-Urban Fault Lines

National averages, however carefully tracked, conceal enormous internal variation — and it is at the state and district level that India's nutrition story becomes genuinely uneven. Rural India consistently shows higher rates of both stunting and wasting than urban India, despite rural areas being the primary focus of flagship nutrition programmes for decades. Several states have moved in the wrong direction even as the national average improved: reporting from Down To Earth, drawing on NFHS-6 district data, found states where underweight prevalence rose from roughly 33% to nearly 40% between survey rounds, and wasting climbed from 19% to 24% — a reminder that national aggregates can mask serious localised deterioration.

Infant mortality shows a comparable rural-urban gap: as of the most recent SRS data, rural IMR stands at 28 deaths per 1,000 live births compared to 18 in urban areas — a gap of ten points that reflects persistent disparities in access to institutional delivery, emergency obstetric care, and postnatal follow-up in rural and remote districts. This unevenness is precisely why national-level success statistics, however genuine, cannot be treated as the end of the story.

A Note on Anaemia

Beyond stunting and wasting, anaemia remains one of India's least-discussed but most consequential nutrition indicators. Earlier NFHS rounds recorded anaemia prevalence above 55% among women aged 15–24 — among the highest levels recorded anywhere — with direct implications for maternal health, birth outcomes, and the nutritional starting point of the next generation of children before they are even born.

Why This Still Matters Economically, Not Just Medically

Chronic childhood malnutrition is not solely a health outcome — it is a long-run economic one. Research consistently linked to stunting shows measurable reductions in cognitive development, educational attainment, and adult earning potential, effects that compound across a lifetime and, at national scale, across generations. A child who is stunted by age two is significantly more likely to underperform in school and earn less as an adult than a well-nourished peer, independent of family income — meaning malnutrition functions as both a symptom of poverty and, left unaddressed, a mechanism that perpetuates it.

For a country whose economic strategy leans heavily on its demographic dividend — a large, young, working-age population — the stakes of getting early childhood nutrition right extend well beyond public health ministries. A workforce that entered adulthood stunted or undernourished is a workforce operating below its productive potential, a cost that shows up in GDP terms even though it originates in a two-year-old's diet.

Read across this table and a consistent pattern emerges: wherever India has built strong health-system infrastructure — institutional delivery, immunisation, emergency obstetric referral — mortality outcomes have improved sharply. Wherever the challenge depends on sustained behavioural change, dietary diversity, and household-level food security — the terrain of wasting and acute undernutrition — progress has been far slower. This is not a coincidence. It reflects the difference between problems that can be solved by expanding a service and problems that require changing food systems, incomes, and feeding practices at scale.

The Way Forward: What Solid Data Suggests Is Still Required

  1. Shift Nutrition Programming to the First 1,000 Days, With Enforcement Behind It

    With only 15% of children aged 6–23 months receiving an adequate diet, the priority is not new schemes but disciplined execution of existing ones — POSHAN 2.0, Anganwadi services, and early breastfeeding promotion — with outcome tracking down to the district level, not just budget disbursement tracking.

  2. Treat Wasting as an Emergency-Response Problem, Not a Chronic One

    Because wasting reflects acute shocks, it needs rapid-response systems — community-based management of acute malnutrition, therapeutic feeding stocked at the primary health centre level, and early-warning triggers tied to seasonal illness and food-price spikes — rather than the slower, infrastructure-based interventions that have worked for mortality.

  3. Close the Rural-Urban and Interstate Gap With Targeted, Not Uniform, Investment

    States and districts where underweight and wasting prevalence have risen despite national improvement need dedicated intervention, not proportional-to-population funding formulas that spread resources evenly regardless of local severity.

  4. Address Anaemia as a Pre-Pregnancy Public Health Priority

    With anaemia prevalence among young women remaining extremely high, iron-folic acid supplementation, dietary diversification, and adolescent nutrition programmes need to reach girls well before their first pregnancy, since maternal nutritional status shapes birth outcomes before a child is even born.

  5. Build Real-Time District-Level Monitoring, Not Just Periodic Surveys

    NFHS rounds arrive roughly every four to five years — too slow to catch localised deterioration before it compounds. Expanding tools like the Poshan Tracker, with verified ground-truthing rather than self-reported Anganwadi data alone, would let policymakers respond to a worsening district in months rather than years.

  6. Sustain the Institutional Delivery and Immunisation Systems That Are Working

    The mortality gains are not guaranteed to hold without continued investment — sustained funding for Janani Suraksha Yojana, Mission Indradhanush, and emergency obstetric referral networks remains essential, particularly to close the rural IMR gap that still sits ten points above the urban rate.

Why This Matters Beyond Health Ministries

India's child health data sits directly in the path of its broader development goals — the same demographic dividend the country is counting on for its economic future depends on the cognitive and physical development of children who are, right now, growing up stunted or inadequately fed in disproportionate numbers. Nutrition policy, in this sense, is not a side conversation to economic policy; it is a leading indicator of it.

The Bottom Line

The honest reading of India's child health data in 2026 refuses easy narratives in either direction. The country has achieved genuine, internationally significant progress on child and maternal survival — gains that reflect real institutional capacity and sustained public investment, and that deserve to be stated plainly rather than buried under a more pessimistic headline. At the same time, nearly a third of India's children remain stunted, its wasting rate remains among the highest recorded anywhere in the world, and the country continues to rank in the "serious" hunger category on the index most widely used to compare nations.

What connects these two realities is a single underlying truth: keeping a child alive and ensuring that child thrives are related but distinct policy challenges, requiring different tools, different timelines, and different kinds of accountability. India has, over the past decade, gotten unmistakably better at the first. The data makes equally clear that the second remains unfinished business — and that finishing it will require the same rigour, the same district-level focus, and the same sustained political attention that made the survival gains possible in the first place.
