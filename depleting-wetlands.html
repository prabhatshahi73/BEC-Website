The Vanishing 'Kidneys': Why India's Wetlands Are Dissolving into Wastelands

From Bengaluru's Bellandur to the East Kolkata Wetlands, rapid urbanisation, unchecked encroachment, and systematic neglect are wiping out these vital ecosystems, leaving cities vulnerable to both drought and deluge.

By Bharat Environment Consultants
August 2026 · 10 min read

Wetlands in India, often described as the 'kidneys' of the landscape for their ability to filter water and regulate hydrological cycles, are facing an unprecedented existential threat. Across the country, from the high-altitude glacial lakes of the Himalayas to the coastal mangroves of the Sundarbans, these crucial ecosystems are being systematically filled, polluted, and built over. Unlike a forest fire or a river turning toxic overnight, wetland loss rarely makes for dramatic news. It happens plot by plot, permit by permit, monsoon by monsoon — until a city that once absorbed its own floodwater discovers, usually during a catastrophic downpour, that the sponge is gone.

Despite being a signatory to the Ramsar Convention and possessing a National Wetland Conservation Programme, India continues to lose its natural wetlands at an alarming rate. Independent research estimates put the loss of India's natural wetlands at roughly one-third of total wetland area between 1940 and 2014 — driven by urbanisation, agricultural expansion, and pollution, and largely unslowed since. A more recent multi-decade review synthesising over a hundred Indian wetland studies puts cumulative degradation even higher, estimating that close to two-thirds of assessed wetlands have suffered measurable shrinkage or quality loss when both natural and human-made wetland types are considered together.

What makes this crisis particularly dangerous is how invisible it is in official accounting. Revenue and land records across most Indian states still classify seasonal wetlands, marshes, and floodplains as "wasteland" — a bureaucratic category with no ecological meaning but very real legal consequences, since land labelled as wasteland is the first to be diverted for housing, industry, or infrastructure. A wetland that floods for four months a year and looks bone-dry the rest of the time offers no visual cue to a district official filling out a land-use form, and that gap between what a wetland actually does and what it appears to be on paper is where most of India's wetland loss originates.

Ground Report: The Urban Collapse

The Bengaluru Example

Nowhere is this crisis more visible than in India's metropolitan hubs. Bengaluru, once known as the City of Lakes, had over 280 lakes in the 1960s, most of them interconnected tanks designed centuries ago to capture and store monsoon run-off across a naturally undulating terrain. Today, fewer than 80 remain, and most of those are classified as sewage-fed rather than rain-fed. The infamous Bellandur Lake, which has repeatedly caught fire due to methane buildup from industrial effluents and untreated sewage, has become a widely cited symbol of urban ecological collapse — an image that has appeared in international coverage of India's water crisis more often than any other single water body in the country.

LAKE UNDER STRESS: Eutrophication on Bellandur Lake, Bengaluru — a lake that receives a large share of the city's untreated sewage and industrial effluent, and has become a recurring flashpoint for toxic foam and fires. Wikimedia Commons

Researchers who have long studied Bengaluru's lake systems point to the same root cause: natural drainage basins and lake catchments have been steadily built over, cutting off the city's own flood buffers. When wetlands and lake beds are treated as "wasteland" available for construction rather than as functioning drainage infrastructure, the result is a city more exposed to both water scarcity and flash flooding — a pattern documented repeatedly around Bengaluru's lake belt over the past two decades. The lakes that remain are frequently interconnected by storm-water drains, known locally as rajakaluves, which were themselves designed to move excess water from one lake to the next during heavy rain. Encroachment on these connecting channels — by both informal settlements and, in several documented instances, by large formal real-estate developments — has severed the network, so that a single blocked drain can now flood a neighbourhood that historically never experienced standing water.

The economic irony is stark: Bengaluru's technology corridor, built on land reclaimed from what was once one of India's densest tank-irrigation systems, now spends increasing sums each monsoon on emergency flood response, road repair, and public health measures linked to waterlogging and vector-borne disease — costs that a functioning wetland network would have absorbed for free.

Kolkata's Natural Sewage Treatment in Peril

On the eastern coast, the East Kolkata Wetlands (EKW) — a Ramsar site of international importance covering roughly 125 square kilometres — serves as the city's natural sewage treatment plant. A vast network of pisciculture ponds known as bheris, along with agricultural fields and settling ponds, the EKW treats a significant share of Kolkata's sewage while sustaining fish farming and livelihoods for thousands of families. The system works on a deceptively simple principle: sunlight and algae in the shallow, nutrient-rich bheris break down organic waste and pathogens, while the resulting nutrient load feeds fish stocks that are then sold in the city's markets — effectively converting a waste-treatment problem into a functioning local economy.

THE CITY'S NATURAL FILTER: The Nalban waterbody within the East Kolkata Wetlands — a Ramsar-designated complex of sewage-fed fisheries and farmland that treats a large share of Kolkata's wastewater at effectively zero infrastructure cost. Wikimedia Commons

Real estate pressure from the expanding city continues to press against this delicate ecosystem despite its Ramsar status. Conservationists have repeatedly warned that any significant loss of EKW's treatment capacity would force Kolkata to build costly mechanical sewage treatment plants to replace a service the wetland currently provides for free — a substitution that, by most engineering estimates for comparable treatment capacity, would run into thousands of crores in capital cost alone, before accounting for the ongoing energy and maintenance expense of a mechanical system that never sleeps and never floods.

"A wetland labelled as wasteland on paper is the easiest land in the country to build on — and the hardest flood buffer for a city to replace once it's gone."
Beyond the Metros: A Countrywide Pattern

From the Himalaya to the Coast

The Bengaluru and Kolkata stories are the most documented, but they are far from isolated. Chennai's Pallikaranai marshland, once a sprawling wetland covering several thousand hectares that buffered the city against both the Bay of Bengal's storm surges and its own monsoon run-off, has shrunk dramatically over the past three decades as it was progressively converted into a landfill, an IT corridor, and residential layouts. The city's catastrophic 2015 flood — described at the time as the worst in a century — is now widely attributed in post-disaster assessments to exactly this kind of cumulative wetland loss across the metropolitan region, compounded by unplanned reservoir releases during peak rainfall.

In Assam, Deepor Beel, a Ramsar site near Guwahati known for its rich fish and bird diversity and as one of the few remaining habitats where wild elephants still visit an urban-adjacent wetland, faces converging pressures from garbage dumping, stone quarrying on its catchment hills, and railway and highway infrastructure cutting through its boundary. In Odisha, Chilika Lake — Asia's largest brackish-water lagoon and a critical wintering ground for migratory birds on the Central Asian Flyway — has required repeated, expensive hydrological interventions to prevent silt-driven closure of its sea mouth, without which the lagoon's salinity balance and fisheries productivity would collapse. In Kerala, the Vembanad-Kol wetland system, the state's largest, continues to lose area to paddy-field reclamation and unregulated houseboat tourism infrastructure, even as it remains central to the state's backwater tourism economy.

Even the high-altitude wetlands of the Himalaya are not immune. Glacial and sub-glacial lakes and high-altitude marshes across Ladakh, Himachal Pradesh, and the northeastern hill states — many unrecorded in any wetland inventory — are experiencing altered hydrology from glacial retreat, road-building through fragile catchments, and, increasingly, unregulated tourism infrastructure clustering around the most scenic sites.

Why Wetlands Keep Disappearing

Four Drivers, One Outcome

Across every region, the mechanisms of wetland loss tend to repeat themselves in a fairly consistent pattern. Encroachment and land-use conversion remain the dominant driver in and around cities, where wetland margins are quietly filled with construction debris until the water body has physically shrunk enough to be reclassified as buildable land — a process local activists in several cities have described as "death by a thousand truckloads." Agricultural expansion plays a comparable role in rural and peri-urban wetlands, where paddy cultivation and aquaculture ponds gradually replace natural marsh vegetation, altering the wetland's hydrology even where the water body itself persists in some form. Untreated sewage and industrial effluent discharge directly into urban wetlands causes hyper-eutrophication — the nutrient overload that produces the toxic foam and fish kills now familiar to residents near Bellandur and dozens of similarly stressed lakes nationwide — while depleting the dissolved oxygen that aquatic life depends on. And invasive species, particularly water hyacinth, choke open water surfaces across lakes, tanks, and reservoirs from Kashmir to Kerala, blocking light penetration, further depleting oxygen, and making both fishing and water extraction physically difficult.

These drivers compound each other. A wetland already stressed by sewage inflow is more vulnerable to water hyacinth colonisation, since the weed thrives precisely in nutrient-rich, slow-moving water. A wetland partially filled for construction loses the depth and flow needed to flush out pollutants naturally, accelerating the eutrophication of whatever water surface remains. By the time a wetland shows visible signs of collapse — persistent foam, fish die-offs, or a fire on the water's surface — the underlying damage is usually the cumulative result of several of these pressures operating together over many years.

The Policy Gap

Legal Ambiguity and Implementation Failure

The primary hurdle in wetland conservation remains the lack of clear, actionable data and consistent enforcement. The Wetland (Conservation and Management) Rules, 2017 were meant to strengthen protection by establishing State Wetland Authorities responsible for identifying and regulating activities within notified wetlands, but they have been criticised for diluting the central oversight that existed under the earlier 2010 rules and shifting primary responsibility to states — many of which lack the resources, mapping data, or political will to enforce them consistently. Notification of individual wetlands under the Rules has, in practice, moved slowly across most states, leaving thousands of ecologically significant water bodies without the specific legal protection the framework was designed to provide.

The definition of a "wetland" itself often creates legal ambiguity in practice. Many smaller water bodies and seasonal wetlands are never formally recorded in revenue or forest records, leaving them without any statutory protection and highly vulnerable to encroachment. This gap has repeatedly ended up before India's courts: the National Green Tribunal and several state high courts have, over the past decade, issued directions in multiple cases involving encroached or degraded wetlands — ordering restoration, halting construction, or directing the constitution of dedicated management authorities. Judicial intervention has become, in effect, a recurring backstop for a regulatory gap that the executive branch has been slow to close on its own, though enforcement of court directions on the ground remains inconsistent, with several high-profile orders still awaiting full implementation years after they were issued.

Environmental policy analysts have long argued that wetland governance in India is too fragmented to be effective — responsibility is typically split across revenue, forest, fisheries, irrigation, and urban development departments, none of which is singularly accountable for a wetland's ecological health. A unified authority that treats wetlands as protected ecological assets, comparable to how forests are governed under the Forest (Conservation) Act, is a recurring recommendation across expert and policy literature on the subject, alongside calls for a legally binding, geo-referenced national wetland inventory that would remove the ambiguity currently allowing so many water bodies to be classified as disposable wasteland.

The Economic Case Nobody Prices In

Part of why wetlands lose so consistently to competing land uses is that their value rarely appears on any balance sheet. A housing project or industrial park has an immediate, quantifiable market value; a functioning wetland's contribution — flood buffering, groundwater recharge, water purification, carbon storage, fisheries support, and climate regulation — is diffuse, delayed, and shared across an entire watershed rather than captured by whoever owns the land. Global estimates of the annual economic value of ecosystem services provided by wetlands run into the trillions of dollars, yet almost none of that value is reflected in the land-use decisions made at the district or municipal level in India, where a wetland's assessed worth is still typically calculated using the same per-acre real estate benchmarks applied to any other parcel of land.

Looking Ahead: The Cost of Neglect

The consequences of wetland depletion extend well beyond biodiversity loss. Wetlands are critical for flood mitigation, carbon sequestration, and groundwater recharge. Their disappearance is measurably reducing the resilience of Indian cities to climate extremes, at precisely the moment when climate projections point to more intense and less predictable monsoon rainfall across most of the subcontinent.

As Chennai, Hyderabad, and Bengaluru have each demonstrated in recent years, the loss of urban wetlands correlates directly with more severe flash flooding during heavy rainfall events. The cost of restoring these ecosystems once degraded is exponentially higher than the cost of protecting them today — restoration of a severely encroached urban wetland typically requires land acquisition, debris removal, sewage diversion, and multi-year ecological rehabilitation, whereas protecting an intact wetland requires, in most cases, simply enforcing rules that already exist on paper.

A functional path forward does not require reinventing India's wetland policy from scratch so much as closing the gap between what the law already says and what happens on the ground:

  • Complete and publish a geo-referenced national wetland inventory, so that no water body can be quietly reclassified as "wasteland" simply because it was never formally mapped.
  • Accelerate state-level notification of wetlands under the 2017 Rules, with fixed timelines and public reporting on notification backlogs.
  • Divert sewage before it reaches the wetland, prioritising interception and treatment infrastructure for the highest-load urban lakes rather than treating the wetland itself as the treatment system by default.
  • Protect and de-encroach connecting drainage channels — the rajakaluves and canals that link individual water bodies into a functioning flood-buffering network — rather than treating each lake as an isolated asset.
  • Price wetland ecosystem services into land-use decisions, so that the flood-mitigation and groundwater-recharge value of a wetland is weighed against development proposals in the same terms as the revenue a project would generate.

India's environmental resilience may depend, in no small part, on how quickly policy shifts from treating wetlands as reclaimable real estate to recognising them as essential natural infrastructure — the kind that, once dismantled, has no affordable substitute.
