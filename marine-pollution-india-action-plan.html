Marine Pollution in India: A National Crisis Demanding a Nationwide Action Plan

India's 7,516-kilometre coastline is being poisoned not primarily by careless swimmers and litterbugs on its beaches, but by a chain of failures that begins hundreds of kilometres inland — in city drains, sewage networks and industrial outfalls that were never built, funded or enforced to stop pollution before it reaches the sea.

By Prabhat Kumar Shahi · Founder, Bharat Environment Consultants 14 min read · [PUBLICATION_DATE]

Walk any stretch of India's working coastline after the monsoon and the evidence is difficult to miss: tangled fishing net, shredded sachets, PET bottles, styrofoam fragments and unidentifiable plastic film, arranged by the tide into long, dark windrows on the sand. It is tempting, and it is the easiest political response, to treat this as a littering problem — to install a few more dustbins, run a beach-cleaning drive, photograph volunteers in gloves, and move on. It is also, according to the government's own data, close to the opposite of the truth. India generates roughly 72,368 million litres of urban sewage every single day, and treats barely 28 percent of it before the rest reaches a river, lake or the sea. It generated 3.47 million tonnes of plastic waste in 2019-20 alone, a figure rising roughly three percent a year. Globally, researchers estimate that around 80 percent of the plastic reaching the ocean travels there via just one percent of the world's rivers — meaning marine pollution is, overwhelmingly, a river and drainage problem wearing an ocean-facing disguise.

This investigation examines what is actually known, and not known, about marine pollution across India's coastal states; why decades of beach-cleaning and awareness campaigns have not solved it; what India's existing legal and institutional framework gets right and where it breaks down; and what a genuinely implementable, measurable national action plan would need to contain. The central finding, drawn from government data, peer-reviewed research and the practices of countries that have already reduced their own marine pollution loads, is straightforward: India cannot clean its way out of this problem after pollution reaches the sea. The national strategy has to shift toward intercepting pollution at its source, along the pathway, before it ever reaches the water.

The Scale, In Verified Numbers

India's coastline has itself become a subject of measurement dispute worth understanding, because it illustrates a broader pattern in this story: official statistics in India are not always consistent, and where they diverge, the discrepancy itself is informative. The long-standing figure, used in most government and CPCB documents, puts India's coastline at 7,516.6 kilometres across nine states and four union territories. A newer Survey of India and National Hydrographic Office remeasurement, using higher-resolution technology, now puts the figure at 11,098.81 kilometres. The difference is not new coastline — it reflects a change in how bays, inlets and small indentations are measured. Both figures are legitimate depending on methodology, but the fact that India's most basic coastal statistic is a matter of ongoing recalculation is itself a signal of how much coastal data infrastructure still needs strengthening.

What is measured with more confidence is the sewage gap. According to the Central Pollution Control Board's most recent nationwide assessment, urban India generates 72,368 million litres of sewage a day; installed treatment capacity covers only 43.9 percent of that; and of the capacity that is actually operational, only around 20,235 MLD of sewage is genuinely treated — roughly 28 percent of the total generated. The remaining 72 percent, in CPCB's own words, is disposed of directly into rivers, lakes and, eventually, coastal waters. Five states — Maharashtra, Uttar Pradesh, Tamil Nadu, West Bengal and Gujarat, several with direct coastlines — account for more than 47 percent of this national sewage load.

On solid waste, the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change told Parliament that India generated 34,69,780 tonnes of plastic waste in 2019-20, up from 33,60,043 tonnes the year before — a compounding annual increase of roughly three percent. Plastic packaging alone, according to industry-reported EPR data, accounted for around 3 million tonnes placed on the market in 2022-23, of which EPR certificates confirm roughly 2.5 million tonnes was processed — leaving a real, if imprecisely quantified, gap between what enters circulation and what is verifiably recovered.

Rivers are the arteries that carry the trash from land to sea.— Boyan Slat, founder, The Ocean Cleanup

On what actually reaches the ocean itself, India's data is honestly thinner, and this gap deserves to be stated plainly rather than papered over with an estimate. Neither CPCB nor NCCR currently publishes a verified, India-specific annual tonnage of plastic or other waste entering the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal. Global figures — such as the widely cited estimate of roughly eight million tonnes of plastic entering the world's oceans annually — are not India-specific and should not be mistaken for one. This is not a minor gap. Without a measured national baseline for marine litter input, it is structurally impossible to set a credible reduction target or verify progress against one. The absence of this number is, in itself, a policy failure the eventual action plan must correct.

The Pathway: From City Drain To Open Ocean

The most important reframing this investigation can offer is about geography. Marine pollution in India is rarely created at the shoreline. It is manufactured in cities — Mumbai, Chennai, Kochi, Visakhapatnam, Surat, Kolkata — and then travels, largely unimpeded, through a chain: household and commercial waste that is not collected or is only partially segregated; stormwater drains that are frequently cross-connected with sewage lines; rivers that carry both treated and untreated flows toward the coast; estuaries and mangrove-fringed deltas that concentrate this pollution during the monsoon; and finally the open coastal waters of the Arabian Sea, the Bay of Bengal and the wider Indian Ocean.

This pathway explains a pattern documented across Indian coastal cities: pollution loads at river mouths spike sharply during and immediately after monsoon rainfall, when stormwater runoff flushes accumulated litter, sediment and untreated sewage overflow downstream in a matter of days. A National Centre for Coastal Research shoreline assessment covering 6,907 kilometres of coast across 11 states found that 2,318.31 kilometres — about a third of the length studied — experienced measurable erosion between 1990 and 2018, a process compounded, though not solely caused, by degraded coastal ecosystems that once buffered wave energy and trapped sediment and debris before it dispersed into open water.

Where India's Coastal Ecosystems Stand

Mangroves, seagrass meadows and salt marshes are the natural infrastructure that would otherwise intercept a meaningful share of this pollution and buffer coastal communities from its effects. India's mangrove cover, per the India State of Forest Report 2023, stands at approximately 4,992 square kilometres — about 0.15 percent of the country's geographical area — and has declined by 7.43 square kilometres since 2021, with Gujarat recording the steepest loss. A 2024 scoping assessment by SaciWaters and the ClimateRise Alliance found that seagrass meadows, covering roughly 516.59 square kilometres nationally, are shrinking under combined pressure from pollution, unsustainable fishing and climate stress, and that more than half of India's mangrove ecosystems are now classified as vulnerable, endangered or critically endangered, with the IUCN specifically flagging South India's mangroves as critically endangered. NCCR's 2024 monitoring additionally documented high microplastic loads inside mangrove sediment itself, with Mumbai's Thane Creek recording the highest concentration studied — evidence that these ecosystems are not merely threatened bystanders but have themselves become pollution sinks.

Who Is Actually Responsible — Beyond The Citizen

It is a persistent and convenient narrative that marine pollution is primarily a discipline problem among ordinary citizens who litter. The evidence points to failures distributed across the entire chain, from production to final disposal, and concentrated most heavily at points where public institutions, not individuals, hold direct responsibility.

At the collection and segregation stage, India's municipal solid waste systems remain inconsistent: source segregation, mandated under the Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016, is unevenly enforced across urban local bodies, meaning recyclable plastic is frequently mixed with organic and other waste before it ever reaches a processing facility, sharply reducing effective recovery rates. At the sewage stage, the CPCB's own data on the gap between installed, operational and actually-utilised treatment capacity shows the failure is not merely inadequate infrastructure but underperformance of infrastructure that already exists — plants that are built but not operational, or operational but non-compliant with discharge norms. Independent assessments of Ganga-front towns found sewage treatment plants meeting only a fraction of their claimed capacity, with disinfection equipment in several Delhi Jal Board-monitored plants failing to meet even basic faecal coliform standards.

At the industrial stage, effluent monitoring in India still relies heavily on periodic inspection rather than continuous, independently verified data, creating room for non-compliance between audits. At the fisheries stage, abandoned, lost or discarded fishing gear — nets, lines, ropes, floats — continues to enter coastal waters with no national deposit, return or gear-marking scheme to track or recover it, contributing to "ghost fishing" that continues killing marine life long after gear is lost. At the ports and shipping stage, India's port waste-reception and reporting systems remain fragmented across individual port authorities rather than centrally tracked. None of this is citizen behaviour. It is institutional and infrastructural.

What India's Existing Framework Gets Right — And Where It Breaks

India is not starting from zero. The Environment (Protection) Act, the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, the Coastal Regulation Zone framework, the Solid Waste Management Rules and, most significantly for this subject, the Plastic Waste Management Rules and their 2022 amendment already constitute a reasonably sophisticated legal architecture on paper.

The 2022 amendment converted Extended Producer Responsibility from a voluntary, CSR-adjacent gesture into a legally enforceable obligation, requiring producers, importers and brand owners to register on a centralised CPCB portal, meet defined collection and recycling targets — including a target of 100 percent plastic packaging collection by 2023-24 and rising recycled-content mandates through 2028-29 — and face environmental compensation under the polluter-pays principle for shortfalls. This is, in design, a credible framework. Independent assessments, however, including a 2024 review by the Centre for Science and Environment and Break Free From Plastic, found that recycling targets under the framework are consistently set lower than collection targets, leaving an unresolved question of what happens to plastic packaging waste that is collected under EPR obligations but never actually recycled — with an estimated 1.7 million tonnes of EPR certificates reported as untraded on the centralised portal, a gap that points to weak downstream verification rather than an absence of rules.

The Coastal Regulation Zone notification, similarly, exists specifically to prevent construction and pollution-generating activity too close to the shoreline and around ecologically sensitive areas — the National Centre for Sustainable Coastal Management has mapped roughly 34,000 square kilometres of Ecologically Sensitive Areas and 12 Critically Vulnerable Coastal Areas nationally under this framework. But CRZ enforcement, like drainage enforcement in landlocked cities, depends on state-level compliance capacity that varies enormously between coastal states, and violations documented by the National Green Tribunal across multiple coastal states suggest the gap is in implementation and monitoring, not in the underlying law.

What Has Actually Worked Elsewhere

India does not need to invent new technology to close much of this gap — several interventions with demonstrated results already exist and, in one important case, are already partially deployed on Indian rivers.

The AlphaMERS case deserves particular emphasis: it demonstrates that river-trash interception is not a foreign technology import problem for India, but a scaling and municipal-adoption problem. Thirty-four installations across eight cities is a pilot scale; India's rivers feeding its most polluted coastal stretches — including the Cooum and Adyar in Chennai, the Mithi in Mumbai, and the Musi in Hyderabad's inland catchment — remain largely without this kind of physical interception at their mouths.

A National Marine Pollution Prevention And Control Action Plan

Based on the evidence above, a credible national response needs to be organised around ten interlocking pillars, each with a measurable target rather than an aspirational statement.

  1. 1

    Intercept pollution before the sea — drain, river and estuary level

    Scale AlphaMERS-style mesh barriers and river-mouth interceptor systems at the outfalls of India's most polluted urban rivers, prioritised using existing CPCB polluted-river-stretch data, with real-time sensors tracking captured tonnage.

  2. 2

    Close the EPR verification gap

    Mandate independent, third-party verification of EPR recycling certificates rather than self-reported PIBO data, and publicly disclose the volume of collected-but-unrecycled plastic packaging waste each year until the current backlog is cleared.

  3. 3

    Close the sewage treatment gap, not just the installed-capacity gap

    Fund operational and maintenance budgets for existing STPs at the same priority as new construction, since CPCB data shows underutilised operational capacity, not only missing capacity, is a major share of the shortfall; mandate real-time discharge quality monitoring at all coastal-city STPs.

  4. 4

    Move industrial effluent monitoring from periodic to continuous

    Require real-time online effluent monitoring at all Red and Orange category industries in coastal districts, with data published on a public compliance dashboard, combined with unannounced physical inspections to prevent monitoring-equipment tampering.

  5. 5

    Introduce a national fishing-gear deposit and recovery scheme

    A refundable deposit on new nets and gear, harbour-based collection and recycling centres, and gear-marking requirements to trace abandoned equipment back to source vessels, reducing ghost-fishing mortality.

  6. 6

    Build a national digital port-waste tracking system

    Centralise currently fragmented port-by-port waste reception and reporting data under one Ministry of Ports, Shipping and Waterways platform, closing the gap between MARPOL reporting obligations and actual verified compliance.

  7. 7

    Set measurable coastal-city performance indicators

    Require every coastal urban local body to report waste collection efficiency, sewage treatment efficiency, drain interception volume and beach litter density on a standard quarterly template, replacing the current inconsistent, self-defined reporting across cities.

  8. 8

    Establish the missing national marine litter baseline

    Commission NCCR and NIOT to run a scientifically designed national marine litter and microplastic monitoring survey and publish the first credible baseline figure for India-specific ocean plastic input — without this number, no reduction target can be verified.

  9. 9

    Strengthen economic instruments

    Expand polluter-pays environmental compensation, introduce deposit-refund systems for high-leakage packaging formats, and price fishing-gear and port-waste deposits to make prevention cheaper than pollution for regulated entities.

  10. 10

    Create a National Marine Pollution Coordination Framework

    Rather than a new institution, formally designate MoEFCC as lead coordinator across CPCB, SPCBs, NCCR, NIOT, the Ministry of Ports Shipping and Waterways, the Department of Fisheries and coastal state governments, with clear, published accountability for who monitors, who enforces, who finances and who publishes data.

Financing: Who Pays, Not A Number Nobody Can Verify

This report does not manufacture a national cost figure for the above plan, because no credible, India-specific costing currently exists in the public domain, and inventing one would violate the same evidentiary standard this investigation applies to every other claim. What is clear is where financing could realistically be drawn from: Union and state budgetary allocation for sewage infrastructure operations, not just capital construction; EPR compliance fees and environmental compensation collected under the polluter-pays principle; port and shipping waste-handling charges; blue-economy financing instruments already being explored under India's broader ocean-economy policy; and, where eligible, multilateral development finance for coastal climate resilience, given the direct overlap between marine pollution prevention and climate adaptation infrastructure. The core economic argument, well established internationally, is that prevention infrastructure is a one-time or recurring operational cost, while pollution damage — to fisheries livelihoods, tourism revenue and public health — is a compounding, indefinitely repeating one.

The Climate Change Connection — And Where The Two Are Not The Same Thing

Marine pollution and climate change are frequently conflated in public discussion, and this investigation deliberately keeps them distinct while explaining how they interact. Marine pollution is a waste-management and governance failure: it is caused by what enters the water, not by the water's temperature or the atmosphere's composition. Climate change is a separate physical process that nonetheless intensifies marine pollution's effects through several concrete mechanisms: more intense, concentrated rainfall events flush a monsoon's accumulated urban litter into rivers and coastal waters in shorter, more overwhelming bursts than infrastructure was designed to handle; storm surges and rising sea levels accelerate the coastal erosion already measured across a third of India's studied shoreline, destroying the natural sediment and debris-trapping function of beaches and dunes; and damage to waste and sewage infrastructure during extreme weather events — as documented following cyclones striking the Sundarbans and other coastal regions — creates acute pollution spikes on top of the chronic baseline problem.

Healthy coastal ecosystems function as the connecting piece between these two crises. Mangroves, a single hectare of which can measurably reduce storm-surge energy across roughly 80 metres of coastline, simultaneously trap sediment and debris, sequester carbon, and provide the nursery habitat that keeps coastal fisheries viable. Their continued decline — 7.43 square kilometres of mangrove lost since 2021 alone — therefore compounds both crises at once: it removes a pollution buffer and a climate buffer in the same stroke. This is why India's MISHTI mangrove restoration initiative and comparable state-level programmes, such as West Bengal's 100-square-kilometre Sundarbans restoration plan, sit at the genuine intersection of marine pollution policy and climate adaptation policy, rather than belonging exclusively to either.

International Lessons, Adapted Rather Than Copied

Singapore's national water agency, PUB, holds unified, island-wide responsibility for drainage, coastal barriers and water quality under a single accountable authority — the same institutional consolidation this investigation's Pillar 10 recommends for India, adapted to India's federal structure through state-level coordination rather than a single national utility. The Netherlands' Bubble Barrier technology works because it was matched to a specific hydrological context — narrow urban canals — and India's equivalent needs matching to its own river geometry rather than uniform replication nationwide. Indonesia's partnership with The Ocean Cleanup on the Cisadane River, aimed at a 70 percent national reduction in marine plastic debris by 2025, demonstrates that a government-set, externally monitored reduction target is achievable for a large, populous developing economy facing comparable governance constraints to India's own — the missing ingredient in India's case is not capacity but the baseline measurement and target-setting step itself.

The Way Forward

The evidence assembled in this investigation points toward one central, defensible conclusion: India's marine pollution crisis will not be solved by better beach-cleaning drives, because the pollution reaching India's beaches and coastal waters is, overwhelmingly, generated and released far from the coastline itself. The sewage gap is a treatment-plant operations problem in inland and coastal cities alike. The plastic gap is a collection, verification and EPR-enforcement problem rooted in municipal and producer accountability. The fishing-gear problem is a missing deposit-and-recovery system. The data gap — the absence of a verified national marine litter baseline — is arguably the most urgent failure of all, because without it, every other intervention in this plan is unmeasurable against a defined starting point.

The philosophy this plan rests on is simple to state and harder, but not impossible, to execute: stop the leakage at its source, monitor the pathway from city to sea, make every polluter along that chain accountable under existing law, recover what nonetheless escapes, restore the mangroves, seagrass meadows and wetlands that would otherwise do this work naturally, and publish every figure so that progress — or its absence — is a matter of public record rather than official claim. India has the coastline, the population depending on it, the legal framework and, as the AlphaMERS example shows, even some of the specific technology already operating domestically. What remains is the institutional decision to measure the problem honestly and fund its prevention with the same seriousness currently reserved for its cleanup.
