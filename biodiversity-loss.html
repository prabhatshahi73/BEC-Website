Biodiversity loss

Biodiversity loss is the decline in the variety of life on Earth, including the number of species, the genetic variety within them and the health of ecosystems. Scientific assessments identify human activity as its main cause.

What biodiversity is

Biodiversity covers three levels: the genetic variety within a species, the variety of species, and the variety of ecosystems such as forests, wetlands, grasslands and coral reefs. Healthy ecosystems supply food, clean water, pollination, flood protection and climate regulation, which are often called ecosystem services.

Drivers of loss

The IPBES Global Assessment of 2019 identified five main direct drivers, in order of global impact: changes in the use of land and sea, direct exploitation such as hunting and fishing, climate change, pollution, and invasive alien species. Behind them are demand for food, energy and materials, and patterns of production and consumption. The assessment reported that around one million animal and plant species face extinction, many within decades, unless action reduces these pressures.

Measuring status

The IUCN Red List classifies species from Least Concern to Extinct, with Vulnerable, Endangered and Critically Endangered as the threatened categories. It is the most widely used measure of extinction risk for assessed species.

India

India is recognised as a megadiverse country and contains parts of four global biodiversity hotspots: the Himalaya, the Indo-Burma region, the Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, and the Sundaland region that includes the Nicobar Islands. The Biological Diversity Act of 2002 governs the conservation of biological diversity and the sharing of benefits from its use.

Responses

The global response is organised under the Convention on Biological Diversity (see Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework). Tools include protected areas, habitat restoration, controls on wildlife trade, and reducing the pressures from agriculture, infrastructure and pollution.
